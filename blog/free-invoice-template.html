A freelance invoice is a record of work you have done and a request for payment. It does not need to look fancy. It does need to be complete, numbered, and easy for a client to approve and pay. This guide walks through how to write one, then points you to a free invoice template you can fill in and save as a PDF.

Start with the parties

Write your name or trading name, a way to contact you, and a postal address. Add the client’s legal or trading name and address, not only a first name in an email thread. If you work under a company, use the company name that matches your bank account. If you are a sole trader, use the name your client already has on the contract or statement of work.

Clients in larger organisations often need a purchase order number, a cost centre, or an accounts-payable email. Ask for those before you send the invoice. Putting them on the document once is faster than chasing a rejected PDF later.

Give the invoice an identity

Use a unique invoice number and do not reuse it. A simple sequence such as INV-0001, INV-0002 is enough. Add the issue date (the day you raise the invoice) and a due date you have already agreed, or a period such as 14 or 30 days from the issue date. If the work was done in a particular month or sprint, say so in the description so the client can match it to their records.

Keep a list of numbers you have already used, even if that list is just a column in a spreadsheet. Gaps are fine. Duplicates are not, because they make it hard to talk about a specific bill months later.

Describe the work in line items

Each line should say what you did, how much of it there was, and the unit price. “Website design, 12 hours at £80” is clearer than “Project work, £960.” If you billed a fixed fee, one line with a quantity of 1 and the agreed price is enough, as long as the description names the deliverable.

Add a discount only if you actually agreed one, and show it as its own figure so the client can see how you got to the total. If you charge tax, show the rate and the tax amount, and say whether the unit prices already include tax. If you do not charge tax, it helps to say “no tax charged” in the notes rather than leaving the tax line blank and unexplained.

Say how to pay you

Put payment details on the invoice itself: bank account name, account number and sort code or IBAN, or the payment link you actually use. If you accept more than one method, list the one you prefer first. Repeat the total due and the due date in the notes so nobody has to hunt for them.

If late fees or a kill fee were in the contract, you can restated the term briefly. Do not invent a penalty that was never agreed. A short thank-you line is optional; a clear payment route is not.

A simple writing sequence

You can write the invoice in this order: your details, the client’s details, the number and dates, the line items, tax and discounts, the total, and the payment instructions. Read it once as if you were the person who has to approve it. Check that the name, the amount, and the due date are obvious without scrolling through a long paragraph.

Then export a PDF and send it in a short email that repeats the invoice number, the total, and the due date. Keep a copy of what you sent. If the client asks for a change, issue a clearly labelled revision or a credit note rather than quietly editing the original number.

Mistakes that slow payment

The usual problems are practical, not typographic. The client’s legal name is wrong. The purchase order is missing. The invoice number was reused. The hours do not match the approved timesheet. The bank details are for an old account. The due date was never agreed and comes as a surprise. Each of those is easier to fix before you send the file than after it has entered an accounts queue.

If a payment is late, reply with the invoice number, the original due date, the amount, and a copy of the PDF. Stay factual. If you need to stand over a contractual late-fee clause, quote the clause rather than inventing a new one in the reminder email.
