End of year tax checklist for freelancers

This end of year tax checklist for freelancers is about getting receipts, expense logs and bank statements in one place before you file. This guide describes a simple system for self-employed people: what to keep, how to organise it during the year, and how to hand it to yourself (or an adviser) when you file. It is about record-keeping, not about which expenses are deductible in your country. For that, use official guidance or a qualified adviser.

What a complete file usually contains

At a minimum, keep a record of what you earned and what you spent on the business, with enough detail to explain a line if you are asked later. That usually means invoices you issued, evidence of payments you received, bills and receipts for expenses, bank and payment-processor statements, contracts or statements of work, and a mileage or travel log if you claim those costs. If you paid contractors, keep their invoices too.

In the United States, the IRS recordkeeping page explains why you should keep records and points to the supporting publications. In the United Kingdom, GOV.UK: self-employed record keeping sets out what records to keep and for how long (for most people, at least five years after the 31 January filing deadline of the relevant tax year). If you are VAT registered, additional VAT records can apply. Read the page that applies to you rather than relying on a blog post for the retention period.

Capture the receipt on the day

The most common failure is not a missing folder in April; it is a coffee-shop receipt that was never photographed in June. When you incur a business cost, save the receipt the same day: a photo, a PDF from the supplier, or an email confirmation. Write the purpose on it if the receipt only shows a total: “train to client meeting, Manchester,” not just a fare.

Store the file in a year folder with a predictable name, such as 2027-06-14-train-manchester.pdf. If you use a notes app or a drive, keep one folder for the tax year and do not mix personal and business scans in a camera roll with no names. A receipt you cannot find is, for practical purposes, a receipt you do not have.

Log it in a list, not only in a folder

A pile of PDFs is not a ledger. Keep a running list with the date, supplier, category, amount, currency, and a link or filename for the receipt. Categories should be boring and stable: software, equipment, travel, professional fees, insurance, phone and internet, office or coworking, advertising, and so on. Do not invent a new category for every purchase.

Do the same for income. Each row can point to an invoice number from your invoice generator and to the payment you received. If a client pays late, or pays two invoices together, the log is how you will later see what happened.

Bank statements are a check, not the whole record

Statements show that money moved. They do not always show why. A card entry called “ACME*1234” will not tell you whether the cost was a business tool or a personal order. Use the statement to reconcile your list once a month: every business line on the statement should have a matching row and a receipt, and every row should appear on a statement or a cash note. Differences are easier to fix in the same month than a year later.

If you mix personal and business spending on one account, the monthly reconciliation takes longer and is easier to get wrong. A separate business account is not a legal requirement everywhere, but it is a practical one.

Mileage, meals and mixed costs

Costs that are partly personal need a note of the business purpose and, where your rules require it, the business share. Mileage should be logged with the date, purpose, and distance as you go, not reconstructed from memory in March. Official mileage rates change; do not copy a rate from an old blog post. Use the rate your tax authority publishes for that year, or the actual-cost method if that is what you have chosen and are allowed to use.

Meals and entertainment rules are specific and often limited. Keep the receipt and the who-and-why note, and follow the official rule rather than a habit you heard about from another freelancer.

A monthly hour, not a yearly crisis

Set a recurring hour each month to import receipts, fill the income and expense lists, and reconcile the statement. If you use the tax set-aside helper, update the income and expense totals at the same time so the amount you reserve tracks the year you are actually having. If you use the 2027 Freelancer Tax & Income Organizer, that monthly hour is the moment you fill the Income, Expenses, Mileage, and Receipts tabs.

Before you file, sort the year by category, add the totals, and set aside the documents an adviser will ask for: the ledger, the receipts, the statements, and a list of questions. Do not wait until the filing deadline to discover a missing month of income from a payment platform.

Digital copies

Many tax authorities allow electronic records if they are complete and readable. That is a reason to photograph paper receipts before they fade, not a reason to throw away a record you have not stored safely. Keep a backup of the folder, not only a copy on one phone. If you change tools, export the list as a spreadsheet so you still have it in a few years, when the retention period may still be running.
